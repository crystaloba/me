Medic: a medication manager for older adults

A medication management app that helps older adults keep track of what they take, understand it, and manage the side effects and mood changes that come with it, without relying on memory alone.

Role
UX Researcher & Designer
Team
Crystal Obaretin, Taya Tepudom, Chia-I Chen
Timeline
3 weeks, research through high-fidelity prototype
Tools
Figma, InVision, Slack

Overview

Medication routines that depend on memory alone

Over a three-week sprint, two teammates and I conducted user research and ideated a healthcare management application aimed at adding efficiency to a user's daily medication routine. I led the research and design work alongside Taya Tepudom and Chia-I Chen, then continued iterating on the design after the initial sprint closed.

Business challenges

  • Reminders go unused

    Many older users don't rely on mobile apps to remind them to take their medication.

  • Knowledge gaps

    Users often don't fully understand the medications they're taking or why.

  • Impersonal telehealth

    Telehealth experiences aren't personalized to the individual user's needs.

Objectives and goals

Build an app that aids and informs users about their medication routine and habits, with a focus on reminder settings, in-app assistance, medication knowledge, and mood and side effect tracking.

Research

What older adults actually struggle with

Ten user interviews were conducted with a population of individuals aged 45 to 70. Before interviewing, we identified the specific challenges this group faces with existing medication and telehealth tools.

  • Operating telehealth

    Older users have trouble operating telehealth services in the first place.

  • Lack of personalization

    Telehealth hasn't become more personalized for the individual user.

  • Inadequate reminders

    Users who had tried medicine apps before felt they didn't receive adequate reminders.

Users have trouble remembering to take their meds on time, and some don't remember taking them at all on a given day. They aren't especially knowledgeable about the medications and supplements they take, but they want to take control of their healthcare, be proactive, and be their own advocate.

Competitor analysis

Medisafe

  • Compiles all of a user's medical and health information in one place
  • Pill and medicine reminders, drug-to-drug interactions, refill alerts
  • Doctor appointments and a health journal with 20+ trackable measurements

MyTherapy

  • Ad-free, award-winning pill reminder and medication tracker
  • Combines a pill tracker, mood tracker, and health journal
  • Helps the user and their doctor track treatment progress over time

Where Medic could stand apart

  • Medical glossary

    A place to locate medication information, drug interactions, and potential side effects without leaving the app.

  • In-app assistant

    A built-in assistant to help with reminders, locating pages, and adding new medications.

  • Mood and symptom log

    A way to jot down notes and track daily mood, side effects, and reactions to meds and supplements.

Process

Discover, define, ideate, design

Problem statement: users need reminders to take their medications and supplements so they can manage their health stress-free.

Solution: a medicine management application that lets users set reminders, manage, and educate themselves on the medications and supplements they take.

  • Discover

    User interviews, competitor analysis, and quantitative research into medication habits

  • Define

    Problem statement, proto-personas, and the core feature set

  • Ideate

    Sitemap, user flows, and low-fidelity sketches

  • Design

    UI style guide and a high-fidelity prototype

Early sketches

Wireframes covered the full loop: onboarding, viewing a medicine's details, logging a dose, adding a new medication, tracking mood in a journal, and a small celebration screen for days when every dose gets taken.

Solutions

Four features built to resolve user needs

  • Customized schedules and reminders

    Reminders that match how someone actually takes their meds

    Users set reminders around their own routine instead of a generic schedule, addressing the core complaint that past medicine apps didn't remind them adequately.

  • Medical glossary

    Answers without leaving the app

    A searchable glossary of medications, drug interactions, and side effects, built for the 80% of users who said they search for this information online instead of asking a provider directly.

  • Journal and mood tracker

    Tracking how a medication actually feels

    Users log daily mood, side effects, and reactions alongside their medication schedule, giving them (and eventually their doctor) a clearer record of how a treatment is going.

  • In-app assistance

    Help finding your way around

    A built-in assistant helps users add new medications and find their way to the right page, aimed squarely at the users who found telehealth tools hard to operate on their own.

UI style guide

A calm, legible system for an older audience

The visual system leans on a single confident blue, a light secondary tint for backgrounds and cards, and Nunito, a rounded sans-serif chosen for legibility across a wide range of ages and reading conditions.

Primary / #2539A5
Secondary / #E0F1FC
Nunito Heading sizes run from 56px down to 20px across six levels, keeping a clear hierarchy on small phone screens.

Major screens

Home, journal, and glossary

Home screen

A 5-day mini-calendar up top, a filter for time of day, and a medicine list with a clear indicator for whether each dose has been taken yet.

Journal

A mood and symptom picker paired with a free-text entry, so a quick tap or a longer note both fit depending on how much someone wants to log.

Glossary

A search bar up top and a set of recommended article tiles below it, covering topics like managing blood pressure and understanding a new prescription.

Navigation

A persistent bottom bar for Home, Medication, Glossary, and Journal, so the four core tasks are never more than one tap away.

Reflection

What I'd do differently

This project was a fast, research-first sprint, and the compressed timeline forced hard calls on what to test and what to take on faith. The quantitative findings, especially how many users had already lost track of a dose or skipped researching a new prescription, did a lot to keep the feature set honest and grounded in an actual problem instead of a nice-to-have.

If I ran it again, I'd want to usability test the onboarding and medication-entry flows directly with people in the 45 to 70 age range we interviewed, rather than relying on the interview findings alone to shape the wireframes. That age range spans a wide range of comfort with mobile apps, and a flow that reads as simple to a younger tester can still trip up the exact users the app is meant for.

I'd also want to pressure-test the in-app assistant concept further. It tested well as an idea in interviews, but assistants are easy to design well on paper and hard to get right in practice, especially for users who already found existing telehealth tools confusing.
