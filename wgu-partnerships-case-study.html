01 Overview

A strong product buried inside
a confusing experience

WGU Workforce Solutions offered genuine value for employers, workforce boards, and community colleges. The UX failed to communicate it. Different audiences arrived at the same page and left without understanding what partnerships included, how employees benefited, or how to take the next step.

Every partner type landed on the same undifferentiated page, faced the same generic content, and hit the same dead-end "Contact Us" button with nothing to justify the inquiry. The gap between WGU's actual product and what partners could perceive online was significant — and costing qualified leads at every stage.

"When a prospective partner asks, 'What do you offer?' — we don't have a consistent answer. Our website shows one thing, our marketing materials say another, and depending on who is pitching, the answer could vary wildly."

— Internal Stakeholder Discovery, WGU Strategic Partnerships
— Three Core Problems

Three distinct failure modes were compounding to suppress partner conversion:

01
No audience differentiation
Employers, workforce boards, and colleges landed on identical pages despite having completely different needs, language, and decision criteria
02
Unclear product architecture
Partners could not tell what they were buying, what employees would experience, or how WGU differed from competing workforce vendors
03
No ROI before the sales call
Decision-makers needed business-case evidence and retention data before they could build internal buy-in — none existed in the experience

— Key Findings
↗ Finding 01
Undifferentiated entry was the primary barrier
All three audience types arrived at the same page. With completely different decision criteria and evidence needs, a single page couldn't address any of them effectively — it was failing everyone at once.
↗ Finding 02
WGU's ROI data existed but was invisible digitally
Strong outcome data, case studies, and retention figures existed in sales decks and internal PDFs — but none of it was surfaced in the digital experience. Partners who needed a business case to justify a conversation couldn't find one.
↗ Finding 03
Competitors were significantly clearer on product
Guild Education and Bright Horizons both offered named tiers, clear feature sets, and outcome data visible before any sales interaction. WGU's experience compared unfavorably to partners who had already evaluated the category.
↗ Finding 04
What partners needed to see
85% needed a clear partnership tier structure. 72% needed employee retention ROI data. 64% needed to understand the downstream employee experience. All three were absent from the original digital experience.

— Constraints & Decisions
Constraints Navigated
  • Three audience types needed fundamentally different experiences without requiring three separate builds
  • Strong ROI data existed in sales decks and PDFs — invisible to any digital visitor
  • B2B decision cycles are long — the experience had to support return visitors building internal business cases over weeks
  • Corporate, academic, and workforce board teams each had different priorities for how the product should be presented
Design Decisions Made
  • Built a shared component system with partner-specific content modules — routing logic at entry determined which appeared
  • Ran a content audit alongside competitive analysis and mapped existing evidence to the moments partners needed it most
  • Designed for progressive commitment: first visit builds credibility, second delivers the ROI case, third triggers the inquiry
  • Used research synthesis to establish a shared problem definition before design — one artefact aligned what meetings could not
04 Solutions

Three features built for
the partner decision journey

Audience-Specific Routing Structured Partnership Tiers ROI Evidence Before CTA Lead Qualification Form Industry Pathway Pages
01
Partner-Specific Routing — Audience Selector at Entry
All three audience types previously landed on the same undifferentiated page. The redesign introduced an audience selector at entry that routes employers, workforce boards, and colleges to tailored journeys — each with distinct messaging, evidence calibrated to their decision criteria, and CTAs matched to their stage in a long B2B buying cycle.
Each partner type now sees the evidence most relevant to their decision
02
Structured Partnership Tiers — Scannable Before Contact
Partners could not tell what they were buying or how tiers differed. The redesign introduced a clear tier architecture with named packages, included features per level, and outcome data tied to each tier — all visible before any sales conversation. Partners could now self-qualify and arrive at the inquiry stage knowing which tier fit their needs.
Product clarity reduced friction and shortened the path to inquiry
03
ROI Evidence Before the CTA — Business Case Built In
Decision-makers could not build internal buy-in without retention and cost data. Retention ROI figures, replacement cost savings, and employee outcome metrics now appear above every primary call to action across all partner journeys — giving champions the numbers they need to sell WGU internally before ever speaking to a rep.

06 Reflection

What I'd do
differently

The clearest challenge in this project was serving three fundamentally different audiences within a single platform without fragmenting the experience or tripling the maintenance burden. The routing logic and shared component system was the right architectural answer — but it required careful discipline. Every content module had to work across partner contexts before we could justify building partner-specific variants on top.

If I were starting again, I would run the content audit in week one, not week six. Discovering that strong ROI data — retention figures, replacement cost savings, employee outcome metrics — already existed in internal sales decks midway through design cost a week of unnecessary stakeholder back-and-forth. That data was the single most valuable design input in the entire project. Finding it earlier would have sharpened the information architecture from the first wireframe.

The other lesson: involve partner representatives in the Define phase, not just Research. Their input on the routing logic and tier naming would have sharpened both much earlier in the process. Research-phase-only involvement meant some Define-phase decisions had to be relitigated once real partners reviewed the wireframes — adding unnecessary revision rounds that could have been avoided entirely.
