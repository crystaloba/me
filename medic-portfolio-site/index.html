Medic 2026 redesign

In 2022 I helped design a medication app for people aged 45 to 70. In 2026 I rebuilt how it looks and how it is organized, and gave it a new job.

Overview

Medic started in 2022 as a three week team project. Two teammates and I interviewed ten people aged 45 to 70, mapped what they struggled with, and designed an app that reminds you to take your medicines, explains them in plain language, and lets you log how you feel.

The research held up. The screens aged less well. When I looked at the 2022 design again, I saw good ideas drawn too small, in a look that could belong to any app.

For the refresh I kept the research, the problem statement, and the four core features. I rebuilt the home screen structure, the look, and nearly every part of the interface. I also added one idea the original never tried. Medic can now sync with a patient portal.

What the 2022 research said

We interviewed ten people aged 45 to 70 and pulled a few numbers from the conversations.

8 of 10

search for medicine information online

6 of 10

had lost track of whether they took a medicine

4 of 10

lack confidence in what they know about their medicines

6 of 10

did no research on a medicine after it was prescribed

The original case study showed these as percentages. Ten interviews is a small group, so the numbers are rough. I show them as counts.

People also told us that telehealth was hard to use, and that medicine apps they had tried did not remind them enough. They wanted to take control of their own care and speak up for themselves.

Users need reminders to take their medications and supplements so that they can manage their health stress free.Problem statement, 2022

That statement still describes the problem. It also sets a bar for the interface. People in this age range need to read it and tap it easily.

What I wanted to fix

I went back through the 2022 screens one at a time. These are the five things I kept running into.

The interface was built small for people who need it big

The tab labels were small. So were the dose check box and the mood icons, and form labels sat on the hairline border of each field. For an audience between 45 and 70, I wanted bigger targets and type everywhere.

Several color pairings were hard to read

The Save button looked disabled while it was active. Gray text sat on lavender fills, and placeholder text was light gray. The yellow filter chip was the only warm color on the screen and it carried no meaning.

The day was laid out as a timeline of times

Nobody thinks "9:30 and 10:00." People think "my morning pills" and "my bedtime pills." The timeline left long empty stretches and made people scan for times.

The Medication tab opened the add form

There was no single place to see everything you take, check how much is left, or edit a medicine without hunting for it on the home screen.

The look was generic

Blue and lavender, illustrations of people, and a mix of Nunito and Poppins. It was tidy and it had no point of view. The product had a wordmark but no logo system and no voice you could point to.

What I kept

I kept the four core features. Those are custom schedules and reminders, the medical glossary, the journal with mood and symptom tracking, and help inside the app. I also kept the dosage help text we wrote in 2022 and the idea that every medicine page shows its forms and side effects.

Goals for the refresh

Make it easy to read and tap

Bigger targets, higher contrast, and labels that stay visible, designed for people between 45 and 70.

Give Medic an identity

A logo, a palette, a typeface, and a character that people can recognize in a screenshot.

Match how people already sort medicines

Organize the day by morning, afternoon, evening, and bedtime instead of by the clock.

Try portal sync

Show how bringing in a medicine list from a patient portal could take the work out of setup.

The idea grew from medicine management to health

Medic started with medicine management. The 2022 problem was simple. People lose track of their doses, so the app reminded them.

The research pointed to more. People wanted to understand their medicines, notice how they feel, and speak up for themselves. Each need became a feature, and the app grew into a place to manage your health.

The pill organizer gave the redesign its structure. You probably know a weekly pill organizer. It has a slot for each day, and many have a row for each part of the day, so you can see what is done and what is left. I used that structure for the whole app. The day splits into four compartments, each with its own soft tint. The idea also shapes the logo, where a capsule replaces the dot of the i, and the buttons, which are rounded like pills.

What changed, screen by screen

Color coding and use of empty space

2022. The home screen listed medicines on a timeline. Most of it sat empty, and finding "my morning pills" meant scanning for times.

2026. The day splits into four color coded compartments with a count of what you have taken, so you find your morning pills by color and position. Space around each dose keeps the screen calm, and a compartment with nothing in it shrinks to one line. A card at the top shows the next dose with one large button. A ring shows how many doses are done. A late dose says "Overdue" in small coral type and nothing more. The week strip became seven capsules, and you can move between weeks.

Adding a medicine without squinting

2022. The form packed every field into one view, with labels sitting on the border of each box. It was compact and hard to read.

2026. The form is one scrolling page with one question per section and labels above the fields. You choose the form from four large tiles. Pills in the bottle and pills per dose each get big plus and minus buttons. Quick buttons set the first dose to morning, noon, evening, or bedtime, and a color tag helps you spot the medicine later.

I kept the help from 2022 and turned it into sheets that slide up. A question mark next to each question opens a plain explanation and an example. The dosage explanation is the text we wrote in 2022.

Adding new medicines and syncing with health charts

2022. The Medication tab opened the form for adding a medicine and nothing else.

2026. The tab now opens with an Add a medicine button. Below it, a card lets you connect your patient portal, also called a health chart, so Medic can bring in your list for you. Your full list follows, with the schedule and how many pills are left. When seven or fewer remain, a "Refill soon" note appears. Marking a dose as taken counts a pill down, so the warning shows up without anyone tracking it.

Quick access to the journal to track your health

2022. Five small mood tiles, light gray prompt text, and a Save button that looked disabled.

2026. The Journal tab sits in the bottom bar, so you can log how you feel in one tap. Six moods fill a grid of large tiles. Each face has its own color, so tired and dizzy do not look alike at a glance. Save stays visibly off until you pick a mood or write a note. The calendar marks days with entries and shows the saved note below.

A glossary that helps you understand your health

2022. A search bar, a featured article, and illustrated tiles with people.

2026. The glossary now teaches in plain language. A bigger search field gets you to a medicine fast. Each medicine page says what the medicine does, shows its forms, and lists side effects and interactions. Side effects reuse the colored faces from the journal, so a symptom looks the same everywhere. A button on each page adds the medicine to your list.

A new identity

The 2022 style guide had two colors and a typeface. The refresh needed more than a repaint, so I wrote full brand guidelines.

Teal does most of the work and carries every main action. Coral appears only where something needs attention, like an overdue dose. Four soft tints belong to the four parts of the day. Open Sans runs the entire interface. Lora is only for the logo, which swaps the dot of the i for a capsule.

The illustrations of people became a character. Cap is a capsule with a face. Cap shows up in the help sheets, the assistant, and the success screen, and never on an error or an overdue dose.

A new idea, syncing with a patient portal

Portal sync was not part of the 2022 research. I added it as a guess. Typing a full medicine list is the slowest part of setup, and many patient portals already hold that list.

The flow has four steps. You pick your health system, sign in on its own page, allow view only access, and review what Medic found. Medic adds nothing until you confirm. Later syncs show only what changed, like a dose that went from 5mg to 10mg. Portals share how to take a medicine but not the times, so Medic picks usual ones and tells you.

The prototype uses sample health systems and no real data. A live version would need registration with each portal vendor, a clear privacy policy, and a real security review. I would not call it available until it works.

Accessibility choices

  • Text meets 4.5 to 1 contrast against its background.
  • Everything you tap is at least 48 pixels tall.
  • Labels sit above fields and stay visible while you type.
  • A larger text setting raises the base size from 16 to 18.5 pixels.
  • Color never works alone. A dose shows an icon and a word, and each mood has a name.
  • Animation switches off when the device asks for reduced motion.

Scope and limits

This redesign did not include new user testing. I refreshed the 2022 design against 2026 best practices for accessibility and mobile design. The 2022 research still supplies the audience and the problem.

What it rests onThe 2022 interviews, plus current guidance on text contrast, tap target size, form labels, motion, and how apps ask for access to health data.
What I did not doRun usability sessions on the 2026 design or compare it against the 2022 version with real users.
How to read the claimsStatements about easier reading and tapping come from design guidance, not from test results.
